Recognizing Phishing Emails

Attackers send forged messages pretending to be banks, HR, college portals, or tech support to steal credentials or trigger malware.

  • Sender Display name looks legitimate, but actual address uses a lookalike or unrelated domain.
  • Urgency Artificial deadlines like "Account suspended in 2 hours" to rush mistakes.
  • Greeting Generic greetings ("Dear Customer") instead of your actual name or account context.
  • Attachments Unexpected .html, .iso, .zip, or macro-enabled files.

Spotting Fake Websites

Phishing sites clone login pages to harvest passwords and session tokens in real time.

  • Typosquatting Subtle misspellings like micr0soft-login.com vs login.microsoft.com.
  • Subdomain Trick Attackers put brand names in subdomains: google.com.security-check.xyz (actual domain is security-check.xyz).
  • HTTPS Myth A padlock or https:// only means encrypted traffic—attackers can get free TLS certificates too.
  • Broken UX Missing links, poor alignment, or asking for OTP/PIN on an unexpected page.
URL Check Habit:

Always inspect the domain right before the first single slash /. When in doubt, open the official site via bookmark or search—not email links.

1. Urgency & Fear

Creates panic so targets act fast without verifying—such as fake legal notices, blocked cards, or immediate password expiry.

2. Authority Impersonation

Pretends to be an IT admin, manager, law enforcement, or executive asking for quick compliance or confidential info.

3. Greed & Bait

Uses fake rewards, refunds, job offers, or "free" software/upgrades to lure users into clicking malicious links.

4. Pretexting

Builds a fabricated scenario (like a delivery issue or IT ticket follow-up) to make a request feel routine and normal.

5. Quid Pro Quo

Offers a service or favor in exchange for information—such as "IT helpdesk" asking for credentials to fix an issue.

6. Spear & Clone Phishing

Tailors messages using personal/organizational details or resends a legitimate-looking email with a swapped malicious link.

Everyday Prevention Checklist

  • MFA Enable authenticator-app or hardware-key Multi-Factor Authentication on critical accounts.
  • Passwords Use unique, strong passphrases and a password manager—never reuse passwords.
  • Hover & Verify Hover over links before clicking to preview the real destination URL.
  • Out-of-Band Confirm unusual payment, credential, or urgent requests via a trusted phone call or known channel.

If You Clicked or Replied

  • Step 1 Change your password immediately from a trusted device and known official website.
  • Step 2 Sign out of active sessions / revoke suspicious app tokens and review recent login activity.
  • Step 3 Run a malware scan if a file was downloaded or executed.
  • Step 4 Report the phishing message to your IT/security team or email provider so others stay protected.
